8. Notes

1 Describe the original image. What kind of pattern or structure does it show?
2 With Z smaller than the image dimensions, how does the drawdown compare to the original? What information is lost?
3 What happens to the threading when Z increases? Do the shaft patterns become more complex?
4 Compare the argmax and Hungarian algorithm results at the same Z. How different is the drawdown? Is one noticeably closer to the original?
5 How does the tie-up change when Z is small vs. large? What does its structure tell you about the image?
6 Choose a colour scheme that you feel best shows the pattern in your image. Describe your choices and why they work.
Things to try:
  • Set Z = 1. The entire image is reduced to a single shaft and treadle — what does the drawdown look like?
  • Set Z equal to the smaller image dimension. Does the drawdown perfectly reproduce the image?
  • Draw a simple shape (checkerboard, diagonal stripe) in the matrix editor and see how SVD factors it.
  • Try the Hungarian algorithm on a large image — how much slower is it, and is the improvement visible?
  • Scale the image to 50% before factoring. How does the lower resolution affect the draft?
  • Edit individual pixels after scaling to fix details, then recompute the draft.
What to look for:
  • Singular values: The SVD assigns each shaft/treadle pair a "weight" (singular value). Large Z captures more weight and more detail.
  • Threading pattern: Each shaft corresponds to a right singular vector — a characteristic pattern across the warps. Shafts with higher-index singular values capture finer detail.
  • Tie-up complexity: A complex tie-up means many shaft/treadle combinations are needed. A simple image may have a nearly diagonal tie-up even at large Z.
  • Quantisation loss: Binarising continuous SVD values always loses some information. The gap between drawdown and original is this quantisation error.
